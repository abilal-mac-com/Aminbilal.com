Understanding

You can outsource your thinking but you cannot outsource your understanding.

kache (@yacineMTB) · February 2026

Amplified by Andrej Karpathy, who credited its author — and to whom the line is already widely misattributed.

In my whole life, I have known no wise people (over a broad subject matter area) who didn’t read all the time — none, zero. You’d be amazed at how much Warren reads — and at how much I read. My children laugh at me. They think I’m a book with a couple of legs sticking out.

Charles T. Munger · Poor Charlie’s Almanack, 2005

Look for people who have lots of great questions. Smart people are the ones who ask the most thoughtful questions, as opposed to thinking they have all the answers. Great questions are a much better indicator of future success than great answers.

Ray Dalio · Principles, 2017

Markets and Money

Everything is liquid at the right price.

Trading-floor adage · origin untraced

Kin to the older dealers’ saying that everything is for sale at the right price; no coiner found as of 25 July 2026.

The market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent.

A. Gary Shilling · by 1986

In print by 1986 and closely associated with Shilling, its likely coiner; routinely lent to Keynes, an ascription for which the evidence is very weak.

The investor who says, “This time is different,” when in fact it’s virtually a repeat of an earlier situation, has uttered among the four most costly words in the annals of investing.

Sir John Templeton · 16 Rules for Investment Success, 1993

The famous “four most dangerous words” is the popular compression; this is the documented sentence. See the essay This Time Is Different.

In the short run, the market is a voting machine but in the long run, it is a weighing machine.

Warren Buffett, after Benjamin Graham · Berkshire Hathaway letters, from 1987

Security Analysis (1934) calls the market a voting machine rather than a weighing machine — never the couplet; the short-run/long-run form is Buffett’s, credited by him to Graham’s classroom.

We simply attempt to be fearful when others are greedy and to be greedy only when others are fearful.

Warren Buffett · Letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders, 1986

The first written form; the bare imperative “be fearful when others are greedy…” is the later popular compression.

It’s only when the tide goes out that you learn who’s been swimming naked.

Warren Buffett · Letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders, 1992

Resurfaced in the 2001 and 2007 letters; usually quoted in paraphrase.

“How did you go bankrupt?” “Two ways. Gradually and then suddenly.”

Ernest Hemingway · The Sun Also Rises, 1926

Mike Campbell’s answer to Bill Gorton; “gradually, then suddenly” is the popular trim.

They mistook leverage for genius.

Steve Eisman · 2018

On Wall Street before 2008; kin lines circulate unattributed — “you thought you were buying genius; all you got was leverage.”

The whole secret to winning big in the stock market is not to be right all the time, but to lose the least amount possible when you’re wrong.

William J. O’Neil · How to Make Money in Stocks, 1988

Being wrong is acceptable, but staying wrong is totally unacceptable.

Mark Minervini · in Jack D. Schwager’s Stock Market Wizards, 2001

The line is the interviewee’s, not the interviewer’s — habitually credited to Schwager, whose book records it.

I never buy at the bottom and I always sell too soon.

Baron Rothschild’s recipe, retold in Reminiscences of a Stock Operator · Edwin Lefèvre, 1923

Reminiscences is Lefèvre’s fictionalisation of Jesse Livermore, to whom the book is habitually misattributed; its narrator retells the old Bourse anecdote.

There is a powerful case for the market mechanism, but it is not that markets are perfect; it is that in a world dominated by imperfect understanding, markets provide an efficient feedback mechanism for evaluating the results of one’s decisions and correcting mistakes.

George Soros

His Popperian case for markets — error-correction, not perfection; the sentence circulates across his own quote records without a settled first locus, as of 25 July 2026.

Work and Leadership

The supreme art of war is to subdue the enemy without fighting.

Sun Tzu · The Art of War

A common English rendering; Lionel Giles (1910) has “supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy’s resistance without fighting”.

Remember too that your time is your one finite resource, and when you say “yes” to one thing you are inevitably saying “no” to another.

Andrew S. Grove · High Output Management, 1983

The sad news is, nobody owes you a career. Your career is literally your business. You own it as a sole proprietor. … You need to accept ownership of your career, your skills and the timing of your moves. Nobody else can do that for you.

Andrew S. Grove · Only the Paranoid Survive, 1996

Abridged.

Find something you enjoy doing and give it everything you’ve got, and the money will take care of itself.

Peter Lynch · Learn to Earn, 1995

It doesn’t make sense to hire smart people and then tell them what to do; we hire smart people so they can tell us what to do.

Steve Jobs

The circulated polish; the documented statement is his 1996 Fresh Air interview — “we hire people to tell us what to do … their job is to figure out what to do and tell us”.

There’s a temptation in our networked age to think that ideas can be developed by email and iChat. That’s crazy. Creativity comes from spontaneous meetings, from random discussions. You run into someone, you ask what they’re doing, you say “wow,” and soon you’re cooking up all sorts of ideas.

Steve Jobs · in Walter Isaacson’s Steve Jobs, 2011

On the design of the Pixar building.

History

History never repeats itself, but it rhymes.

Modern adage · commonly misattributed to Mark Twain

Earliest strong match: Theodor Reik, 1965. Twain, who died in 1910, was first credited in 1970.

History never repeats itself, but the Kaleidoscopic combinations of the pictured present often seem to be constructed out of the broken fragments of antique legends.

Mark Twain & Charles Dudley Warner · The Gilded Age, 1874

What Twain actually wrote — the sentence the adage is forever mistaken for.

Those who cannot adjust to change will be swept aside by it. Those who recognize change and react accordingly will benefit.

Jim Rogers · A Gift to My Children, 2009

Not one country in existence today has had the same borders and government for as long as two hundred years. The world will continue changing.

Jim Rogers · A Gift to My Children, 2009
